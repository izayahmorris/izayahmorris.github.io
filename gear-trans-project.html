Cycloidal Reducer Trebuchet Project

10:1 3D‑printed cycloidal speed reducer for automated trebuchet reloading

MECH 996 · Gearing & Transmission Design · NEMA 17 · Onshape · PLA FDM

Jump to Overview

Project Overview

This project applied cycloidal speed reducer theory to a practical system: a desktop trebuchet that automatically reloads its counterweight using a compact 10:1 transmission.

A NEMA 17 stepper motor provides high‑speed, low‑torque input. The cycloidal reducer trades speed for torque, enabling reliable lifting of 40–400 g counterweights while keeping the mechanism small enough for a tabletop demonstration.

Design & Implementation

Transmission Design

  • Generated cycloidal profiles from epicycloid equations using a custom FeatureScript.
  • Selected 11 fixed pins and 10 lobes to achieve a 10:1 reduction ratio.
  • Iterated from an open housing to a fully constrained “sandwich” architecture to control axial motion.

Trebuchet Integration

  • Coupled the reducer output to a reloading spool driving the counterweight via cable.
  • Modified an existing desktop trebuchet design with a locking hook, guide roller, and optimized counterweight basket.
  • Controlled the NEMA 17 via Arduino for repeatable reloading cycles and test conditions.

Testing & Results

Reducer Performance

  • Built a dedicated test stand with a 200 mm torque arm and digital scale to measure output torque.
  • Measured ≈97.5 N·cm at the output versus 650 N·cm theoretical, corresponding to ≈15% mechanical efficiency.
  • Ran a clearance study on multiple cycloid discs; a 0.65 mm pin clearance gave the best balance of smooth motion and torque capacity.

Trebuchet Launch Testing

  • Tested counterweights from 40–400 g with a 16 mm marble projectile on a desktop range.
  • Recorded launch distances from roughly 7–38 in and fit an energy model to the data.
  • Back‑calculated an overall system efficiency of ≈50% from counterweight energy to projectile kinetic energy.

Key Learnings & Future Work

What I Learned

  • Material stiffness and manufacturing tolerances dominate real‑world efficiency in precision transmissions.
  • Axial constraint is critical in cycloidal drives; small misalignments can cause binding and severe losses.
  • 3D‑printing artifacts can introduce directional friction, leading to asymmetric behavior in forward vs. reverse rotation.
  • Rapid PLA prototyping enables many design–build–test cycles, at the cost of ultimate mechanical performance.

Next Steps

  • Rebuild critical components in stiffer polymers such as nylon or carbon‑fiber‑reinforced nylon.
  • Add rolling pins and bearings to promote true rolling contact between lobes and pins.
  • Apply lubrication or surface treatments and refine tolerances to close the gap to theoretical efficiency.
