Cycloidal Reducer Trebuchet Project

10:1 3D‑printed cycloidal speed reducer for automated desktop trebuchet reloading

Cycloidal Drives Gearing & Transmission Onshape PLA FDM Trebuchet Design Experimental Testing

Project Overview

For my MECH 996 Gearing & Transmission Design final project, I designed, fabricated, and tested a 10:1 cycloidal speed reducer integrated into a desktop trebuchet to automate counterweight reloading.

A NEMA 17 stepper motor supplies high‑speed, low‑torque input. The cycloidal reducer trades this speed for torque, enabling reliable lifting of 40–400 g counterweights while keeping the mechanism compact enough for a tabletop demonstrator.

The project combined analytical design of the cycloidal profile, iterative 3D‑printed prototyping, and experimental torque and range testing of the full trebuchet system.

Design Theory & Methodology

Cycloidal Drive Fundamentals

The reducer uses an epitrochoid‑based cycloidal disc rolling against a fixed ring of pins. With 11 housing pins and 10 lobes on the disc, each full rotation of the eccentric input advances the disc by one lobe spacing, yielding a 10:1 reduction ratio.

Profiles were generated in Onshape using a custom FeatureScript that implements the parametric cycloid equations, allowing quick iteration on lobe count, eccentricity, and pin geometry.

Figure: Cycloidal disc profile and pin housing generated parametrically in Onshape.

Design Evolution

Early Iterations

  • Phase 1: Large 80 mm base radius design with long print times and excessive material usage.
  • Phase 2: Scaled‑down housing, but open‑back design allowed axial translation and binding.
  • Multiple input shaft concepts, including split shafts that were hard to rework between iterations.

Final Architecture

  • “Sandwich” assembly with the cycloidal disc constrained between front and back plates.
  • D‑shaft input directly mates to the NEMA 17 output for better concentricity.
  • Six output pins drive a winding spool that pulls the trebuchet counterweight via cable.

Design Specifications & Implementation

Experimental Testing & Results

Reducer Torque Testing

To quantify reducer performance, I built a dedicated test stand that fixed the housing and attached a 200 mm torque arm to the output. A digital scale at the arm tip measured force while the NEMA 17 drove the input at 500–1000 rpm.

Measured Torque & Efficiency

  • Theoretical output torque: ≈650 N·cm (65 N·cm × 10).
  • Measured output torque: ≈97.5 N·cm at slip onset.
  • Resulting reducer efficiency: ≈15% under load.
  • Best performance achieved with 0.65 mm clearance between disc lobes and pins.

Trebuchet Launch Testing

After integrating the reducer, I evaluated full system performance by measuring launch distance for counterweights from 40 g to 400 g.

  • Launch ranges spanned roughly 7–38 in as counterweight increased.
  • An energy‑balance model fit to the data indicated ≈50% overall system efficiency from counterweight potential energy to projectile kinetic energy.
  • Range gains plateaued beyond ~200 g, showing diminishing returns with heavier counterweights.

Design Challenges & Key Learnings

Material Compliance & Efficiency Gap

The ≈15% measured reducer efficiency was far below the 85–95% range reported for metal cycloidal drives. PLA’s low modulus allowed the disc and housing to deflect under load, shifting contact from pure rolling to partial sliding and dramatically increasing losses.

Friction & Directional Behavior

3D‑printed layer lines introduced anisotropic surface roughness. The reducer ran smoothly in the forward direction but exhibited audible catching and no sustained torque in reverse, indicating directional friction tied to layer orientation.

Importance of Axial Constraint

Early open‑back designs allowed the disc to translate under load and bind. The final “sandwich” housing highlighted how critical axial constraint is for maintaining proper cycloidal engagement and avoiding catastrophic jamming.

Rapid Prototyping Trade‑offs

PLA FDM enabled over 50 design–print–test cycles in about four weeks, which was invaluable for learning. The trade‑off was reduced stiffness and surface quality compared to machined metal components, limiting achievable efficiency.

Future Work & Skills Developed

Future Improvements

  • Reprint critical components in Nylon or carbon‑fiber‑reinforced nylon for higher stiffness.
  • Replace fixed output pins with rolling elements or bearings to promote true rolling contact.
  • Add lubrication or surface treatments to reduce plastic‑on‑plastic friction.
  • Refine tolerances and consider randomized layer start positions to reduce directional artifacts.

Technical Skills

  • Parametric cycloidal drive design and gearing analysis.
  • Onshape FeatureScript for automated profile generation.
  • FDM design for manufacture, tolerancing, and assembly.
  • Experimental torque and range testing with custom fixtures.
  • System‑level efficiency analysis and loss breakdown.
